# Assignment 4 — Reflection

Throughout Assignment 4, one of the biggest challenges our team faced was figuring out how to turn our original startup idea into a functional proof of concept. Our initial direction was to develop a lending platform that could provide more personalized loan recommendations by considering both traditional credit scores and borrowers' financial behavior. We wanted to demonstrate that bank statement data could provide additional information about a borrower's repayment capacity, potentially helping individuals whose credit scores do not fully represent their financial situation.

However, as we started working on the machine learning component, we realized that finding a suitable dataset was much harder than expected. To properly evaluate our original hypothesis, we needed a dataset containing three things for the same borrowers: credit scores, bank transactions, and actual loan default outcomes. We explored several datasets, including LendingClub, Prosper, Bondora, and synthetic credit scoring data. Although some contained credit scores and default information, they generally lacked detailed bank transaction histories. For example, Bondora included income, debt-to-income ratios, and available cash, but these variables were not sufficient to analyze actual cash-flow patterns.

We initially trained and compared several machine learning models using Bondora, including Logistic Regression and Random Forest. However, the results were disappointing, with ROC-AUC values around 0.53–0.56. More importantly, we realized that even if we improved the model's accuracy, it would not necessarily validate our main idea because the dataset did not contain the transaction-level information we needed. This was an important turning point. Instead of continuing to optimize models on unsuitable data, we decided to reconsider the scope of our proof of concept.

Eventually, we switched to the Berka dataset, which contains historical bank transactions linked to loan outcomes. Although it does not include traditional credit scores, it allowed us to build a model based on actual cash-flow features, including monthly inflows, outflows, net cash flow, income stability, and account balances. After comparing different models, we selected a cash-flow-only Logistic Regression model based on its cross-validation performance. It achieved a cross-validated ROC-AUC of approximately 0.843 and a held-out test ROC-AUC of 0.757. While these results were encouraging, the small sample size and historical nature of the dataset mean that the model is not yet suitable for real lending decisions.

Another challenge was integrating the model into our application. A model working in Google Colab is very different from a model that can process an uploaded CSV through a web interface. This required us to think carefully about feature consistency, input validation, and how the frontend, backend, and prediction logic communicate.

The biggest lesson we learned from this assignment is that building a proof of concept is not just about achieving good model performance. It is about identifying what can realistically be implemented and validated with the resources available. Although we had to narrow our original vision, our current prototype demonstrates a working cash-flow analysis pipeline and provides a foundation for future credit-report integration. Going forward, our priority is to obtain more representative data, validate the model further, and eventually test whether combining credit information with cash-flow features improves default prediction.
