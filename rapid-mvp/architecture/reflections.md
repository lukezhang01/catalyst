## From a Local Prototype to a Production-Grade System

The feedback from Assignment 2 fundamentally changed our perspective on the Classroom Presentation Randomizer. Initially, our team focused primarily on building a functional prototype, demonstrating its core features, and identifying limitations and potential future improvements. However, feedback from our instructor highlighted a critical weakness in our presentation: we were explaining what our product could do and what it still lacked, rather than convincing our audience why they should use it. This prompted us to shift our focus from technical implementation to customer value.

Our Customer Journey (CUJ) analysis further reinforced this transition by encouraging us to consider the product from the user's perspective. For students and instructors managing classroom presentations, the primary challenge is not simply randomizing teams. It is organizing presentations fairly, reducing administrative effort, and ensuring that sessions run smoothly. A useful product must therefore address the entire experience, from preparing teams to managing presentation time. This changed our definition of success: a working randomization algorithm alone was no longer sufficient; the product needed to solve a real problem in a convenient and reliable way.

In response, we evolved our initial locally runnable prototype into a deployed web application. The original prototype established the core functionality, including team randomization, preloaded presentation teams, a team dashboard, and a presentation timer. Building on this foundation, we focused on making the application accessible and practical beyond our development environment. Deploying the application through Vercel allowed us to move toward a shareable, browser-based experience, reducing the need for users to set up and run the application locally.

More importantly, we began approaching our product as a solution rather than a technical demonstration. The randomizer's value lies in combining several classroom tasks into one streamlined workflow. Instead of manually selecting teams, tracking presentation progress, and monitoring time separately, instructors can use a single interface to manage these activities. Features such as the presentation timer, two-minute warning, and Q&A countdown help users maintain a consistent schedule, while the team dashboard makes presentation information easier to access. Together, these features support a more organized classroom experience and reduce the effort required from instructors.

The peer feedback also changed how we communicate our progress. Rather than emphasizing limitations and listing features that might be developed in the future, we now prioritize the problem, our solution, and the benefits users receive. This does not mean ignoring technical limitations. Instead, we distinguish between improvements that are necessary for the current user experience and ideas that can be explored later. Our presentation should demonstrate the product's value through its actual workflow and features, allowing the audience to understand its usefulness rather than simply hearing us describe it.

Ultimately, the A2 feedback and CUJ findings helped us recognize that developing a successful product requires more than writing functional code. It requires understanding users, prioritizing their needs, and delivering an accessible experience. The transition from a local prototype to a deployed application represents an important step in this process. Although the system still has opportunities for improvement, our focus has shifted toward building and communicating a product that users can understand, access, and benefit from, rather than presenting a prototype defined by its limitations.
